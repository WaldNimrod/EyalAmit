## More than learning an instrument

### Working with breath through the didgeridoo

The didgeridoo is usually introduced as an ancient wind instrument.

In my work, I see it first and foremost as a **breath-training instrument**.

Playing requires coordination between breathing, lips, tongue, cheeks, throat and attention. The instrument provides immediate feedback. When the breath changes, the sound changes.

This makes the didgeridoo an unusually direct tool for exploring the way we breathe.

My approach is active.

You are not simply receiving a treatment while someone else plays. You learn, practice, listen, adjust and gradually develop skills you can continue using independently.

The goal is not musical performance.

**The instrument is the practice tool. The breath is the work.**

# The cbDIDG Method

## A structured method developed through more than two decades of practice

Over the years I developed **cbDIDG**, my method for working with breathing through the didgeridoo.

The method grew gradually from several worlds that became connected through my own experience:

* more than 25 years of playing and teaching didgeridoo

* my background as an electronics engineer
* my interest in physiology, acoustics and the mechanics of breathing
* thousands of hours of individual work with students and clients

Three principles are central to the method.

### Active work

The person participates in the process.

The work is learned through practice rather than received passively.

### The didgeridoo is the tool, not the goal

Learning to produce sound, coordinate the breathing system and eventually develop circular breathing creates a practical framework for exploring breath patterns, control, awareness and attention.

You do not need to become a musician.

### Change develops through repetition

This work is not built around a one-time experience.

Skills develop through guided practice, repetition and gradual independent work.

# Why breath became my life's work

My relationship with breathing began long before I understood that it would eventually become my profession.

When I was twelve, the apartment building where I lived in Givatayim caught fire. A friend from the building died in that fire, and the experience stayed with me.

Years later, during my military service, I developed severe asthma and allergies.

For a long time I lived with wheezing, medication, inhalers and the feeling that breathing itself had become difficult.

Then I encountered the didgeridoo.

At first, I was simply drawn to its sound.

I wanted to understand how this strange instrument could produce a continuous, deep tone, so I began learning to play.

Only gradually did I begin to notice what the practice was asking me to do.

Slow down.

Coordinate.

Listen.

Pay attention.

Develop control without force.

And, most importantly, build a completely different relationship with my own breath.

My personal experience with asthma and breathing was one of the forces that led me to study the subject more deeply.

I do not present my experience as a medical promise for someone else.

But it shaped the question that has guided my work ever since:

**What can happen when a person learns to work consciously, patiently and consistently with their own breathing?**

That question eventually became the foundation of my professional work.

# Private Sound Healing

## A different way to experience the didgeridoo

Sound healing is a very different experience from active breath work.

During a private didgeridoo sound session, you do not need to learn, play or practice.

You lie down and receive the sound.

The low drone, harmonics, rhythms and physical vibration of the didgeridoo create an immersive listening experience that many people experience as relaxing, grounding and meditative.

Sessions are available for individuals and couples.

### Breath work or sound healing?

The distinction is important.

**Didgeridoo breath work is active.**
You learn, practice and gradually build skills.

**Sound healing is receptive.**
You listen and experience the sound.

Both involve the didgeridoo, but they are two very different forms of work.

# What does research say about the didgeridoo?

Scientific research into didgeridoo-related practices is still limited.

It is therefore important to distinguish between promising findings and established medical evidence.

Several studies are particularly interesting.

## Snoring and obstructive sleep apnea

A randomized controlled trial published in the *British Medical Journal* examined adults with snoring and moderate obstructive sleep apnea.

Participants in the didgeridoo group received lessons and practiced regularly for four months.

Compared with the control group, the didgeridoo group showed improvements in:

* daytime sleepiness
* apnea-hypopnea index
* their partners' reported sleep disturbance

The study included only 25 participants, so the findings are promising but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence.

## Stress and relaxation

A randomized study involving 74 university students compared a 30-minute live didgeridoo sound meditation with silent breath meditation.

Both groups reported increased relaxation.

Participants in the didgeridoo meditation group experienced significantly greater relaxation and lower acute self-perceived stress than participants in the silent meditation group.

## Mood, stress and autonomic regulation

Another study followed 20 beginners through ten didgeridoo-based health promotion lessons.

Researchers reported changes in measures related to:

* mood
* mental stress
* pulse
* salivary amylase
* autonomic nervous system balance

These studies are part of a relatively small body of research.

They do not establish didgeridoo practice as a replacement for medical treatment, but they do support continued investigation into the instrument as a tool connected to breathing, sound and wellbeing.
